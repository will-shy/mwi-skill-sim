How this works

Simulating one trial

Everyone assigned to a trial shares the same skill, so they share the same interval and tick in lockstep. That means within one tier (a fixed room level), each member's expected contribution per tick is constant — no need to roll dice tick by tick: 

workPower       = floor(level × (1 + efficiency))
perMemberTick   = successRate(level, roomLevel) × (1 + doubleChance) × workPower
successRate     = max(minSuccess, clamp01(baseSuccess × (1 + levelBonus)))
levelBonus      = (level − roomLevel) × bonusAbove   if level ≥ roomLevel
                  (level − roomLevel) × penaltyBelow if level < roomLevel
tier HP         = progressMult × roomLevel × (1 + attendancePct × rosterSize)
ticksNeeded     = ceil((tier HP − progress) / sum(perMemberTick over roster))

Instead of looping tick-by-tick, each tier is solved directly for how many ticks it needs, so simulating a full 3600s trial costs roughly one division per tier (a few dozen), not one per tick (hundreds). This is a deterministic expected-value approximation — real play has RNG variance around it — but with up to 22 concurrent actors over many actions it stays tight, and it's what makes evaluating thousands of candidate rosters during optimization fast enough to be interactive.

Why members can be worth benching (the attendance tax)

Tier HP scales with roster size (1 + attendancePct × rosterSize), and that cost is flat per attendee — independent of how strong the joining member is — while their benefit (their own contribution rate) scales with their own level. Roughly, adding a member is only worth it if their own contribution is more than about attendancePct of the team's existing total throughput. On the real roster this rarely bites at the default 1% (nearly everyone clears that bar), but it becomes a real trade-off at higher attendance costs: testing at 30% attendance benched 6–7 of the guild's lowest-level members for these skills, because their attendance tax outweighed their contribution once success rate and roster share were both accounted for. That's why the optimizer includes a prune move (drop a member if the trial scores higher without them), on top of adding and swapping.

Assigning members across trials (greedy + local search)

Assigning up to 86 members across 4 trials (≤22 each) to maximize total tiers cleared is a partition problem with a roster-size-penalized, diminishing-returns objective — not something worth an exact solver for a planning tool this size. Three local-search moves, run in rounds until nothing improves:

  • Greedy fill — repeatedly assigns whichever (member, trial) pair gives the single largest score gain, and only accepts moves with a strictly positive gain (it won't force-fill a trial with someone who'd hurt it).
  • Prune — for each trial, drops any member whose removal would raise that trial's score.
  • Swap — for every pair of trials, swaps two members across them if it improves the combined score.

The score being optimized is tiersCleared + fractional progress into the current tier (not raw tier count), since raw integer tiers give the search no gradient to climb between tier boundaries. One consequence: prune/swap can raise this continuous score while occasionally landing on one fewer integer tier than plain greedy fill — the two metrics aren't quite the same thing, and a small continuous improvement can trade a completed tier for more progress spread across the other trials.

How close is this to the true optimum? (benchmarked against exact search)

The real 86-member problem can't be solved exactly — assigning each member to one of 4 trials or leaving them out is 586 possibilities (≈1060) by brute force, or 386 (≈1041) with the smartest known exact technique (dynamic programming over subsets) — both far beyond any computer, which is exactly why this uses a heuristic instead of an exact solver.

As a sanity check, the same DP was run to true optimality on a reduced 14-member slice of the real roster (small enough to be exact: 314 ≈ 4.8M states). Result: DP-optimal scored 20.8152 (continuous) in 8.5s; this page's heuristic scored 20.8135 in 4.8ms — about 0.01% off the true optimum, roughly 1,760× faster (and it happened to land on 19 cleared tiers vs. the DP's 18, illustrating the continuous-vs-integer-tier point above). That gap is expected to stay small at the full 86-member scale too, though it can't be proven exactly since nothing can solve that instance exactly.
